# Economic Writing Principles

**Canonical writing rules for the section-writing skills.** Built from the craft canon
— McCloskey *Economical Writing*, Cochrane "Writing Tips for PhD Students", Shapiro
"Four Steps to an Applied Micro Paper", Head "The Introduction Formula", Bellemare,
Nikolov (IZA DP 15057), Goldin–Katz. Examples come from papers *you* like, collected once at
setup with the `add-exemplars` skill. The section-writing skills (`write-intro`, `write-litreview`, `write-data`,
`write-design`, `write-results`) point here; do not restate these rules elsewhere.

**Controlling style sources:** Cochrane, "Writing Tips for Ph.D. Students" (2005), and the house
usage rules in §1b. Where those two disagree, the house rules win. Where either conflicts with an older source in this canon, §10 records the ruling.

---

## 0. The writing process (Shapiro's Four Steps)

1. **Aspirational introduction.** Write the intro for the paper you aspire to write; make up results within reason. Test: "if I write the paper outlined in this introduction, will I be happy with it?"
2. **Research.** Start with the aspect most likely to kill the project. "Return frequently to the introduction. It is your compass."
3. **Robot.** Write the body for a robot: don't persuade, just state assumptions, methods, findings. Linear (never use an undefined concept), plain ("the robot does not understand fancy talk and is not impressed by it").
4. **Contractual introduction.** Rewrite the intro as a contract: the reader agrees to be excited provided the paper delivers what the intro promises. Every intro claim maps to a table/figure that cashes it.

## 1. General prose principles (McCloskey-heavy)

1. **Writing is thinking.** If a passage won't write clearly, the idea isn't clear yet; fix the idea first.
2. **One point per paragraph, stated in the first sentence.** If you can't write the paragraph's point in one marginal clause, split or cut.
3. **The "so what" test.** Delete any paragraph that only exists because you did the work.
4. **No boilerplate.** No throat-clearing openers ("Economists have long been interested in…"), no "This section discusses…". Delete the first paragraph of any early-draft section and check whether anything is lost.
5. **No table-of-contents paragraphs inside sections.** Headings carry structure. (Intro roadmap is the one contested exception — see §3.8.)
6. **No elegant variation: one thing, one name, throughout.** If "treatment effect," "impact," and "influence" all mean the same estimand, pick one. Same for sample names, group labels, variable names.
7. **Verbs over nominalizations; active over passive.** "I collected data," not "Data was collected." Search "-tion of," "is/are + past participle."
8. **Concrete short words.** "use" not "utilize," "to" not "in order to," "whether" not "whether or not."
9. **Minimal jargon, no invented acronyms.** Any word you don't read in a newspaper is suspect.
10. **A naked "this" is a bug.** Every "This shows…" gets a noun: "This coefficient," "This restriction."
11. **Kill intensifiers, and put no adjectives on your own work.** Search-and-destroy: very, quite, clearly, obviously, interestingly, importantly, striking(ly), reassuring(ly), novel, of course. The allowance is zero, headline included: the number carries the point. No double adjectives either.
12. **Emphatic word at the end of the sentence.** End on the number or the finding, not a citation or qualifier. Vary sentence length around a short mean.
13. **Revise by ear.** Read aloud; where you stumble, rewrite.
14. **Clarity is an ethical duty.** Unclear prose either hides a weakness or wastes the reader's time.
15. **Formulaic reporting language is fine; repeated content is not.** "Column (2) reports…" can repeat forever; an *idea* stated twice loses one instance.
16. **Present tense, formal citations.** "Oster (2012) finds…", "I show…" — never "I will show" or "we showed."
17. **Footnotes only for what the typical reader can skip:** long reference lists, simple algebra, documentation. If a point matters, it goes in the text; if it does not, delete it. No caveats, reconciliations, priority claims, or parenthetical comments below the line. The same rule covers parentheses in the text: evidence addresses stay, asides go.
18. **Triage: one paper, one point.** Write the one-paragraph contribution statement first; everything that doesn't serve it goes to the appendix or dies.
19. **Write for a skimming reader.** Abstract + first two pages + headings + table titles must convey question, answer, and method.
20. **"I" is fine; no royal "we."** Use "I" on a sole-authored paper and "we" on a co-authored one, or to mean "you, the reader, and I." Not "one can see" and not a passive.
21. **What first, then why, then alternatives,** at every scale. "I adjust income by the square root of household size." Next comes why the adjustment matters, then other adjustment functions. Never open with the purpose clause.
22. **No previews or recalls.** "As we will see in Table 6," "recall from Section 2," "I show below," and stage maps signal the wrong order. Fix the order.
23. **Nothing before the main result that a reader does not need to understand it.** Two things pass the test in an empirical paper: the raw figure of the identifying variation and the identification argument. Everything else follows the result or moves to an appendix.
24. **Take responsibility for each sentence.** Subject, verb, object. Delete "it should be noted that," "it is easy to show that," "a comment is in order," and whatever else precedes the real claim. Track what the reader knows; never use a term before defining it.

## 1b. Usage and mechanics

House usage rules, except where marked. A revision pass checks every line.

**Claims**

- Spell out every comparison. "More accurately" than what?
- Avoid assertions: cite almost every factual claim, even in the introduction.
- Avoid advocacy: no "I hope to show." State what you find.
- Attribute italics in quotations: "(emphasis in original)" or "(emphasis added)."

**Register**

- No contractions. Write "and," "or," and "200 or more," not "&," "/," and "200+."
- No comma splices. Punctuation goes inside quotation marks.
- "Data are"; "dataset is." "Its" is possessive; "it's" means "it is." "Cannot" is one word. "Et al." has one period.
- "That" restricts; "which" adds. "The variables that came from X" implies other variables exist.
- "Fewer" for anything countable in principle; "less" or "amount of" otherwise.
- "Between" takes exactly two items; use "among" for more.
- "US" or "U.S." as a noun; only "U.S." as an adjective.
- Capitalized Republican, Democrat, Liberal, and Conservative name parties; lower case carries the literal sense.

**Numbers**

- Write out any number that needs no hyphen (nine, fifteen, fifty), and use digits for the rest (23). Estimates, percentages, quantities with units, exhibit and column references, and mathematics take digits.
- Never start a sentence with a number.
- Keep the leading zero: 0.42, not .42.

**Compounds**

- Avoid nouns used as adjectives ("grammatical error," not "grammar error"). Established technical terms such as "treatment effect" and "event study" are exempt.
- Hyphenate a compound modifier before its noun, not after: "race-based policy," but "the policy is race based." Adverbs ending in "-ly" take no hyphen: "equally weighted index" (Cochrane).

**Smaller habits** (Cochrane)

- "In which" for models; "where" for places.
- Spell out authors' names, never "FF."
- Name a Greek letter when you reuse it: "the elasticity of substitution θ."
- Describe a model's structure without "assume"; save "I assume" for claims about the real world.
- One direction is enough for a causal link: no "up (down) … down (up)."
- Sparing italics, no cute opening quotation, no "illustrative" empirical work.

**Format**

- 12-point Times New Roman, one-half spacing, one-inch margins, unless a journal or reader asks otherwise.
- Everything must read on a black-and-white printer (§7.10).

## 2. Abstract

1. Write it last, after the contractual intro is stable.
2. State the question, the answer with magnitudes, and how you learned it — not the topic. Ban "This paper studies…" as the load-bearing sentence.
3. Be specific, not promissory: name the estimand, the source of variation, and the headline number or direction.
4. ~100–150 words. Cut secondary results and caveats first.
5. No literature in the abstract (Cochrane). Say what you find, not what you look for.

## 3. Introduction

Backbone: **opening paragraph (one fact, the question, the headline) → design → remaining results in order of importance → closest papers and contribution → optional roadmap**, ~5–7 paragraphs (longer when the paper does several distinct things). Head's formula supplies the opening grounds and the contribution test; Cochrane sets the order: the contribution comes before the literature.

1. **Open with at most one concrete fact, on one of four grounds** (Head): Y matters; Y is puzzling; Y is controversial; Y is big/common. Cite the fact (§1b). No statement of how important the topic is and no discussion of the literature. If the contribution can carry the first sentence, start there (Cochrane).
2. **Two hook sins:** bait-and-switch (promise interesting, deliver boring) and "all my friends are doing it" (motivating by the existence of other papers).
3. **The question and the headline land in ¶1,** the question clean enough that the paper can answer it.
4. **Give the fact behind the headline, not just the conclusion** (Cochrane): estimand, magnitude, and units. Caveats, threats, and robustness stay out of the intro.
5. **Closest papers after the headline:** the two or three prior papers whose incompleteness your paper repairs. Everything else gets at most a sentence, with any long reference list in a footnote.
6. **Value-added: ~3 contributions stated relative to those closest papers.** Head's test: the *question* must be understandable from the hook alone; a *contribution* should only make sense in light of prior work — if it's intelligible without the literature, it's actually the question.
7. **The intro is a contract** — line-by-line, every claim maps to the exhibit that cashes it.
8. **Roadmap: contested.** Head/Nikolov: keep it, short, with project-specific landmarks. Cochrane/McCloskey: cut it. Resolution: if it survives find-and-replace onto another paper, delete it. (Section-handoff sentences at the end of each section are the alternative.)
9. **Length: about three pages is the recommendation** (Cochrane), not a hard cap. Past three pages, check whether the literature or robustness leaked in; keep the extra only if the paper does several distinct things.
10. **Write the intro first, edit it every session** (Head: "If you can't write a good introduction, then you may be writing the wrong paper").

## 4. Literature / positioning

1. **Cochrane's rule always holds:** the contribution comes first, then the two or three closest papers, set off so that an uninterested reader can skip them. The home is the end of the intro. A titled "Related Literature" part there is optional; an untitled paragraph is equally fine. Never pages of literature at the top of the intro.
2. No standalone "Literature Review" section and no journal-survey coverage of a field.
3. The job is positioning, not coverage: distinguish the paper from the two or three closest papers; give credit for anything that might otherwise seem new.
4. Generous, non-insulting: criticize a *specific incompleteness your paper fixes*, attributed to the approach/data, never the authors.
5. Position mostly against peer-reviewed economics.
6. Keep a literature spreadsheet (study, journal/year, identification, results, caveats, relation) and write the positioning paragraphs from it.

## 5. Data

1. ~1 page main text (2–3 when the data construction *is* a contribution); gore to the appendix with a one-sentence pointer.
2. Description checklist: source, period, structure, unit of observation, N, the central variables — space allocated by importance.
3. State limitations against the ideal dataset; major ones in text with a robustness exercise after the main result, minor ones in the appendix. Never let a referee find a limitation you didn't flag.
4. Summary stats: means/SDs for every analysis variable, by relevant subgroups (treatment/control, pre/post).
5. Descriptives do work: each reported fact is used later; orphaned descriptives get cut.
6. Sample construction is part of the section: screens, reasons, counts dropped at each step.
7. Reproducibility test (Cochrane): a fellow graduate student can reproduce every number from the instructions in the paper and its appendices.
8. Keep here only what the reader needs before the main result; other descriptives follow the result or move to the appendix.

## 6. Research design / empirical strategy

1. 2–3 pages; split into estimation strategy (equation, estimator) and identification strategy (why causal).
2. Write the estimating equation; define every symbol; mark the parameter of interest; state FEs and clustering with one-line justifications.
3. Justify the specification; every right-hand-side choice defensible in one sentence.
4. **Identifying assumption in your setting's words, never textbook form.** Delete any paragraph that would survive transplantation into another DiD paper.
5. Identification here, threat tests later: state the identifying assumption and the error term in this section. Tests of specific threats (reverse causality, OVB, selection, measurement error) follow the main result, each signed and answered with a design feature or a test.
6. One plain-language design sentence before any algebra: "the design compares [these units] to [those units] around [this event]." If you can't write it, the design isn't clear yet.
7. Connect data limitations to identification.
8. Robot rules apply: linear, plain, no rhetoric.
9. In economic terms (Cochrane), say what moves the right-hand variable, what sits in the error term, and why the two are uncorrelated. For every number presented, be able to name the variation that drives it; adding fixed effects changes that variation.

## 7. Results

1. Less is more; every reported estimate serves the main point. Main tables show the parameters of interest, not every control.
2. Order by importance, never by the order you ran things: main result → robustness → mechanisms/heterogeneity.
3. Results-paragraph template: opens with a substantive claim, walks the columns with magnitudes, closes tying back to the hypothesis.
4. **Magnitudes in interpretable units, never just signs and stars.** Statistical significance ≠ economic significance; never "significant" when you mean "large"; distinguish "imprecisely estimated" from "zero." Every important number carries a standard error.
5. **Tables stand alone.** Informative title; regression equation and left-hand variable in the caption; the dependent variable as a column heading, or as a prominent heading if common to all columns; the mean of the dependent variable (and SD where useful) in the footer of each column, or in the notes if common; human-readable labels, never software variable names; SEs vs. t-stats stated; curated notes complete enough for a skimming reader, including how key variables are built; booktabs. No number appears in a table that the text does not discuss; a sentence about a row or a pattern counts. Test: could someone teaching your paper put the exhibit on a slide without confusing the class?
6. **No false precision:** two or three significant digits; round the SE to its first nonzero digit and match the coefficient (4.6 with SE 0.7, not 4.56783 with SE 0.6789); uniform decimals in a column; rescale away leading zeros. Full precision lives in the results log and the CSV.
7. Robustness: acknowledge prominently, present parsimoniously — one summary exhibit or paragraph in the body, full tables in the appendix, never before the main result, never in the intro.
8. Report mixed results honestly and symmetrically; no asymmetric vagueness for unfavorable estimates. (House rule, reinforced: no editorializing point estimates as good/bad.)
9. Prefer a figure for the key result where the design allows (event studies especially): the headline exhibit is the picture of the identifying variation; the table backs it.
10. **Figures stand alone and survive black-and-white printing.** A caption that defines every symbol, labeled axes, sensible units. No distinction carried by color alone, in papers or slides: every series also differs by marker or line style, every bar group by hatching. No dotted lines that vanish when reproduced; no dashes on volatile series.

## 8. Conclusion

1. Do not restate the findings (Cochrane): one statement each in the abstract, the intro, and the body is enough.
2. A paragraph or two: limitations, then implications beyond the paper, with caution proportional to identification. No future-research plans and no speculation.
3. Normative restraint: policy claims must follow from the estimates with the external-validity caveat attached.
4. A short conclusion is fine and often best.

## 9. Shared defaults

Patterns that strong applied-micro papers share. Treat them as defaults, not options,
and check them against your notes on papers you like. Where a default conflicts with §§1–8,
§§1–8 win.

1. **The headline magnitude appears in ¶1 of the intro**, always with its base: "level change (percent of mean) after k years," a range ("5 to 10%"), or a concrete counterfactual person or move. Exception: a compact null-result intro may defer numbers but states direction and design in ¶1.
2. **The identifying assumption gets one plain-English sentence**, in the setting's own nouns, often stated twice, verbatim (intro + strategy section).
3. **Sign the bias, always.** Never "may be biased" — say which way: toward zero, or an overestimate. The direction is the argument.
4. **Voice the killer objection in your own words after the main result**, then dispatch it: "One may worry that…", "A concern for our analysis is…", "These differences could reflect… or could reflect…". First explain what you do; defend it later.
5. **Raw data before regressions only when it shows the identifying variation.** That one figure or two-group means table may precede the workhorse spec (§1.23); other descriptives follow the main result.
6. **Falsification logic before falsification result:** "Finding positive lead coefficients would cast doubt on…" — pre-trends and placebos framed as tests the design could have failed.
7. **Every coefficient is converted**: to percent of a stated mean, an IQR contrast, dollars, a share of an aggregate, or a variance-explained. A number never sits uninterpreted. The denominator is defined once in the text and reused.
8. **Nulls are never naked**: say whether the zero is precisely or imprecisely estimated, and give the ruled-out effect size scaled by the mean ("we can rule out a reduction of more than X; the mean is Y"). Load-bearing nulls are tied to the theory they kill.
9. **Robustness is compressed**: one clause or sentence per check with the moved coefficient and an appendix address; cross-design agreement is itself the robustness argument.
10. **Verdict-first topic sentences carry the number, not an adjective**: "A one-SD higher exposure lowers output by X percent of the mean," not "The effects are large." Section-ending synthesis sentences state what was learned before moving on; subsection headers can assert findings ("Sorting Does Not Explain the Effect").
11. **Screens carry their rationale inline**, with the cost reported (how many units each screen drops). Arbitrary cutoffs are called arbitrary and bracketed with alternatives.
12. **Constructed variables are taught by worked example** with small integers or one real record traced through every coding decision — the single most stealable data-section device.
13. **Linkages are narrated with match rates + external benchmark + validation check**, algorithm gore exiled to the appendix.
14. **Prior work is never trashed.** The formula: [what the prior approach achieves] + [the one thing it cannot do] + [we do it]. Disagreement targets a belief or an approach, never an author; differing results get a proposed reconciliation in the text. Agreement is corroboration.
15. **Enumerated scaffolds everywhere**: "Two main challenges arise… First… Second…"; threats named and numbered; mechanisms as named hypotheses with testable implications stated *before* the table, and a verdict sentence after.
16. **Evidence addresses travel with claims**: "(column 6 of Table 3, significant at 1 percent)" — parenthetical, so sentences stay declarative.
17. **The evidence carries the interest; no flourishes.** Calibrated hedges: "arguably exogenous," "suggestive evidence," interpretation one notch below the evidence.
18. **What first, then why, for methods:** "I estimate X. The specification addresses Y because…", not "To address Y, I estimate X" (§1.21).

## 10. Explicit disagreements in the canon

- **Roadmap paragraph:** Head/Nikolov keep (short, specific); Cochrane/McCloskey cut. Test: a roadmap that fits any paper fails both camps.
- **Intro opening:** Head opens on a hook and reaches the question by ¶2–3; Cochrane opens on the contribution. Ruling: at most one concrete fact, then the question and headline in ¶1.
- **Citations in the intro:** Head and Cochrane keep literature out of the opening; the house usage rules require citations for almost any claim. Ruling: the house rules. Cite every factual claim, but do not discuss the literature there.
- **Literature placement:** Head puts antecedents before the value-added; Cochrane puts the literature after the contribution and keeps it to the closest papers. Ruling: Cochrane always, at the end of the intro; a "Related Literature" title is optional.
- **Intro length:** Top-journal practice can run long; Cochrane's upper limit is three pages. Ruling: three pages as a recommendation, not a cap.
- **Hyphens:** Cochrane allows omitting them; the house usage rules require them before the noun. Ruling: the house rules.
- **Table notes:** Cochrane would leave out variable construction; the house usage rules call descriptive notes essential. Ruling: the house rules, with the notes curated.
- **Footnotes:** Many published papers use them as a pressure valve; Cochrane does not. Ruling: Cochrane.
- **Conclusion:** Bellemare's formula includes a summary and future research; Cochrane cuts both. Ruling: Cochrane.
- **Order within a passage:** Many published papers lead with the purpose; Cochrane leads with the action. Ruling: Cochrane.
- **Before the main result:** Published papers often show raw data and dispatch threats first; Cochrane admits only what the reader needs. Ruling: Cochrane's test, with the raw figure of the identifying variation and the identification argument passing it.
- **Adjectives on your own work:** Published papers spend an occasional "strikingly"; Cochrane spends none. Ruling: Cochrane.
- **Talks:** Cochrane's seminar advice bans motivation, literature, and result previews; Shapiro's talk canon uses all three in compressed form. Ruling: the slide canon stands (`econ_slides_principles.md` §2).
- **Robustness visibility:** Nikolov allows a named body section; Cochrane pushes tables to the appendix with one line. Common ground: mention in body, details out of the reading path, never in the intro.
